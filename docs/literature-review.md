# Literature Review — Why This Architecture, Not Another One

Short answer up front: the case brief asks for a **constraint-based recommender that explains itself, on a thin real-world dataset, where the explanation is the deliverable and the AI must never be the decision-maker**. Every one of those four constraints maps onto an active, specific line of research — not onto vibes. This document narrows from the general recommender-systems literature down to the exact gap this project sits in, then makes the comparison explicit in a table.

## 1. Recommenders, broadly — and why "just embed and rank" isn't the standard answer here

Recommender systems are conventionally split into collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, and knowledge-based approaches [1]. Two-sided marketplaces that connect customers to service providers — home-service platforms like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack are the closest commercial analogue to an event-contractor catalog — depend on exactly this kind of match-recommendation policy to convert browsing into bookings, and this is still active operations-research territory: Shi (2024, *Management Science*) shows the *optimal* match-recommendation policy for such a platform has a simple, efficiently computable structure once conversion rates are known [5]. That is the economic argument that "recommend up to 3 contractors well" is a real, studied problem with real value attached to solving it — not a toy warm-up exercise.

## 2. Narrowing to knowledge-based / constraint-based — because our data is thin and our failures must be honest

Collaborative filtering needs interaction history; content-based filtering needs rich item features and a lot of items. We have neither at scale: 66 real profiles, no purchase history, no ratings. This is exactly the regime knowledge-based and constraint-based recommenders were built for — reasoning over explicit attributes (city, budget, format, availability) rather than learned interaction patterns, which is precisely why they don't suffer the cold-start problem the way collaborative approaches do [1]. Felfernig & Burke's foundational review of constraint-based recommenders goes further and states the property our system leans on hardest: a constraint-based recommender can **explain a recommendation even in situations where no solution could be identified** [2]. That single sentence is the academic justification for why `none_fit` and `no_category` are first-class response states in our API, not error cases bolted on afterward.

## 3. Narrowing to the event/wedding-vendor domain — where this exact problem has already been attempted, and the gap is visible

This isn't a hypothetical domain. Kesuma et al. (2020) built a content-boosted collaborative-filtering recommender specifically for wedding-service vendors, and reported accuracy falling from 84% for established users to 54% for new users [3] — a direct, published demonstration of the cold-start problem in *this exact domain*, with the same "thin catalog, sparse history" shape our 66-profile dataset has. Singh, Singh & Bathla (2020) built a hybrid content+social recommender for event planning, mining vendor reputation signals out of review text [4]. Both are real prior art for "recommend event vendors" — and both optimize a numeric similarity/accuracy score, with the natural-language justification either absent or a byproduct rather than the product. That's the gap this case brief explicitly targets ("the value is in the explanation, not the sorting").

## 4. Narrowing to LLM-generated explanations specifically — a genuinely underexplored corner, not a solved problem

A 2025 systematic review of using LLMs to explain recommendations screened 232 articles published since ChatGPT's release and found only **six** that actually used an LLM to generate explanation text for an existing recommendation, as opposed to using the LLM to produce the recommendation itself [6]. That's a striking number: it means "LLM explains, doesn't decide" is still a minority pattern in the literature, not a mature, solved technique we're merely reapplying. Building the case's matching pipeline this way is a deliberate bet on a specific, recent, and still-thin line of research — which is also exactly why it needed the extra guardrails described next.

## 5. Narrowing to why the AI is validated, not trusted — the hallucination-safety argument

Unconstrained LLM explanation text is a known hallucination risk: recent surveys on retrieval-grounded and verified generation catalogue this as an open, actively-worked problem across 2024–2026 [7], and cold-start recommendation work specifically flags LLM-generated content as needing grounding against the actual catalog before it can be trusted [1]. The emerging answer in adjacent high-stakes domains is a neuro-symbolic split: a deterministic/symbolic component owns the decision, an LLM only narrates it in natural language, and a verification layer sits between the two. In structural engineering, exactly this split (deterministic external algorithms handle the critical computation, the LLM only enriches and explains) was reported to reach ~94% accuracy while keeping response latency under two seconds [8]. Our system applies the same split to a completely different domain: Python's filter-and-score engine is the sole decision-maker; Groq only rewrites the explanation text of the top-3 cards *after* they're already chosen, and that text is checked against real contractor IDs and field values before being shown — with an automatic deterministic-template fallback if the check or the API call ever fails.

## Why this matters (the short version)

Recommend-and-explain for service marketplaces is a real, economically-motivated problem [1, 5]. Thin, attribute-rich, high-involvement catalogs like ours specifically call for constraint-based reasoning over collaborative filtering [1, 2]. The exact domain has prior art that hits a well-documented cold-start wall and treats explanation as secondary [3, 4]. Using an LLM purely to explain an already-decided recommendation is still a small, recent slice of the literature [6] — and the reason it's small is that doing it safely requires exactly the validation/fallback layer that adjacent high-stakes domains have only just started formalizing [7, 8]. This project sits at that intersection on purpose, not by accident.

The pattern in that last row doesn't exist as a single system anywhere in the six rows above it — it combines the constraint-based "explain the failure too" property [2], the domain-specific cold-start honesty of [3], and the validated-LLM-narration safety pattern that so far has only been demonstrated in an unrelated domain [8]. That combination, applied to a real 66-profile event-vendor catalog, is the actual contribution.
